The five shortcuts

On a Mac the important key is Cmd, the one next to the space bar with the little loop on it. Hover over a card and watch the keys press themselves.

Cmd+Space
Find anything

Opens Spotlight. Start typing the name of an app, a file, anything. This is the big one. Learn only this and you're ahead.

Cmd+C/V
Copy and paste

Select something, Cmd C to copy it, click where you want it, Cmd V to paste. Same two keys in every app there is.

Cmd+Tab
Swap between apps

Hold Cmd, tap Tab to move along the row, let go. Much faster than hunting in the Dock.

Cmd+Shift+4
Screenshot a bit of the screen

Drag a box over whatever's confusing you. It lands on your desktop. Then paste it into Claude and ask what it means.

Cmd+Q
Properly quit an app

The red dot only hides the window. Cmd Q actually closes it. If something's playing up, Cmd Q then open it again fixes it most of the time.

fn+fn
Talk instead of type

Tap the fn key twice and start talking. Bonus sixth one, and probably the one you'll use most.

Making it yours

All of this is in System Settings, the grey gear. Open it with Cmd Space, type settings, hit enter. There's a search box at the top left of it, which is the easiest way to find anything in there.

  1. WallpaperSystem Settings, then Wallpaper. Pick anything. Or use a photo of your own from Photos. This is the one that makes it feel like your machine and not a loaner.
  2. Dark mode, if you want itAppearance, then Dark. Easier on the eyes at night. Try both, keep whichever you like.
  3. Make the text biggerDisplays, then choose a Larger Text option. Zero shame in this, half the people I work with run it. Also Accessibility, then Display, has a bold text switch that helps a lot.
  4. Sort the Dock outDesktop & Dock. Make the icons bigger. Turn on Magnification so they grow when you point at them. Drag any app you don't use off the Dock and it vanishes in a puff of smoke, which never stops being satisfying.
  5. Turn on dictationKeyboard, then Dictation, switch it on. Now double-tapping fn anywhere lets you talk instead of type.
The mouse thing

Mac trackpads scroll the opposite way to what you might expect. If it feels backwards, it's in Trackpad, then Scroll & Zoom, then turn off Natural scrolling. Also worth turning on Tap to click in that same spot so you don't have to press down every time.

The two patterns

Pattern one
The App Store

Blue shopping bag in the Dock. Search, press Get, done. It installs itself and lands in your Applications.

Safest option. Apple has checked everything in there. If a thing you want is in the App Store, get it from the App Store.

Pattern two
Download from the web

You download a file ending in .dmg. Double click it and a window opens showing the app's icon and a shortcut to your Applications folder.

Drag the icon onto the Applications folder. That's the install. Then eject the little disc that appeared on your desktop, and bin the .dmg file. That's it.

The rule that keeps you safe

Only ever download from the maker's own website. Chrome from google.com, Claude from claude.ai. Never from a link in an email, never from a site offering it free that normally costs money, and never from an ad at the top of search results.

If the Mac warns you that something can't be opened because it's from an unidentified developer, that's the Mac doing its job. Stop, and ask me.

A couple of things people never get told

Apps don't live in the Dock

The Dock is just shortcuts. The actual app sits in the Applications folder in Finder. Dragging it off the Dock doesn't delete it.

To really delete something

Open Finder, go to Applications, drag the app to the Bin. That's uninstalling on a Mac. There's no special uninstaller.

The spinning beachball

Means the app is thinking, not broken. Wait a bit. If it stays, Cmd Q, or Cmd Option Esc to force it.

You can't ask
it wrong.

Everyone's banging on about AI and most of it is rubbish, so here it is straight. It's a thing you type at. It types back. It's not a person, it's not your mate, and it won't fix anything on its own.

What it is: free most of the time, there at 3am, doesn't get bored, doesn't tell anyone, and has no idea what you did last year. That last part matters more than people admit.

Start with the problem, not the tool

There's an old line about this. If the only tool you have is a hammer, everything starts to look like a nail. A bloke called Maslow said it, and it's the single most useful thing to know about any new tool, including this one.

Right now everyone's got a shiny new hammer and is wandering round looking for nails. They ask "what can I use AI for?" and end up doing party tricks. Ask it the other way round and you get somewhere real.

The trap
Starting with the tool

"What can AI do?" leads to writing a poem about your dog, showing someone, and never opening it again.

Nothing changed in your life. That's how most people's AI thing ends, within about a fortnight.

The way round
Starting with the annoyance

"What's shitting me this week?" Then bring that. A form, a bill, a letter, a decision you've been avoiding.

Something actually gets better. And then you use it again on Thursday without being told to.

The same goes the other way

Sometimes the answer is that this isn't the right tool. If it's about a person, go and talk to the person. If it's medical, go to a doctor. If it's legal and it matters, get a real one. Knowing when to put the hammer down is part of the skill.

The four rules, and there are only four

1. Say it however it comes out

Half a sentence is fine. Spelling doesn't matter. Swearing doesn't matter. Talk to it like you'd talk to a mate who happens to know things.

The people getting the most out of this are not the ones writing clever prompts. They're the ones who just say what they actually want.

2. Make it ask you things

This is the one nobody knows. If you can't explain what you want, tell it to interview you instead.

Ask me questions one at a time until you properly understand what I want, then help.
3. Tell it when it's lost you

It has no idea how it's going unless you say. "I don't get that, say it simpler" works every single time, and it never gets shirty about it.

You can also say "too long", "that's not what I meant", or "start again". None of it offends anybody.

4. Keep going in the same chat

It remembers everything within one conversation, so the second answer is usually better than the first, and the fifth is usually better again.

Most people ask once and give up. The good stuff is three messages in.

What a bad prompt actually does

People think a rubbish question gets a rubbish answer. What actually happens is it asks you to fill in the gaps, and answering its questions is a hundred times easier than writing a good prompt in the first place.

  1. You type: "i need to sort my money out"Vague, badly spelled, no punctuation. Perfect. Send it.
  2. It asks: what's the main thing worrying you, do you have debts, are you paid weeklyNow you're just answering questions about your own life, which you're already an expert in.
  3. You answer in whatever words come out"rent keeps catching me out and i get paid fridays". That's a complete answer.
  4. You get something that actually fits your situationWhich no amount of clever prompt-writing would have got you, because it needed to know about the Fridays.

Things to be straight about

It's confidently wrong sometimes

It can state something false in exactly the same tone as something true. If it matters, check it. Ask it "how sure are you about that?" and it'll usually tell you straight.

It's not a person

It doesn't know you, doesn't remember yesterday's chat unless you're in it, and isn't your friend. Useful, not a relationship.

It can't do the turning up

It'll out-think you all day. It cannot turn up when someone's car dies. That's the bit that's yours, and it's getting rarer and more valuable, not less.

The category nobody talks about

There's a whole set of questions people never ask anyone. Not because they're shameful, but because asking means admitting you didn't already know. How does tax work. What does that word on the form mean. How do I write this so I don't sound like an idiot.

Give those to the machine. It frees the humans up for the stuff only humans are any good at, and it has no memory of you being embarrassed.

Stuff for the desk

Kit that's
worth it.

A laptop on its own is uncomfortable to work at for more than about an hour. None of this is urgent and none of it is required, but a few cheap bits make a real difference to whether you want to sit down at it.

Talk to me before you buy anything. Some of it I've already got spare.

In the order I'd buy them

A proper mouse $30 to $70

The single biggest upgrade. Trackpads are fine for a bit, but a real mouse is faster and kinder on your wrist. A Logitech wireless one around the $40 mark is all you need, and get one that takes a AA battery rather than recharging, so it never dies mid-job.

Skip the Apple Magic Mouse. It's $150, it's uncomfortable, and it charges from a socket underneath so you can't use it while it charges. Genuinely a silly object.

A laptop stand $25 to $60

Lifts the screen to eye height so you stop looking down. Your neck will notice within a week. A folding aluminium one from Officeworks or Amazon does the job. Once it's up there you'll want a separate keyboard, so read the next one first.

A keyboard, if you get the stand $50 to $90

Only needed if the laptop's up on a stand, because you can't reach its keys any more. A Logitech K380 is about $59 and pairs to the Mac and your iPhone at the same time. Make sure whatever you get says it works with Mac, otherwise a couple of keys end up in the wrong spot.

A wrist rest $10 to $25

Cheapest thing here and the one people are most surprised by. A foam or gel bar in front of the keyboard so your wrists aren't bent back. Kmart do one for about $12 and it's the same as the $40 ones.

Headphones with a microphone $0 to $80

For our calls, and for talking to Claude without feeling daft. If you've got AirPods or any earbuds already, use those, they'll pair to the Mac by themselves. Otherwise anything with a mic on the cable is fine.

A USB-C hub $40 to $80

Only if you find yourself short of sockets. Gives you USB ports, an SD card slot and an HDMI socket for a monitor. Don't buy one until something you own won't plug in, because you might never need it.

Prices are rough

Those are ballpark Australian prices as a sanity check, not quotes. They move around and they go on special constantly. If something's way outside those ranges, that's the signal to ask me before you buy.

Setting the desk up so it doesn't hurt

Free, takes five minutes, and matters more than any of the kit above. Sore shoulders are the main reason people stop sitting down at the thing.

  1. Top of the screen roughly level with your eyesA stand, or a couple of books. You should be looking slightly down, not craning forward.
  2. Elbows at about a right angle, shoulders downIf you're reaching up to the keys, the chair's too low. If your shoulders are round your ears, it's too high.
  3. Feet flat on the floorIf they dangle, put something under them. A box works.
  4. Every twenty minutes, look at something far away for twenty secondsThat's the whole trick for sore eyes. Out the window at a tree is ideal.
